# Today's Question

What evidence would convince a school district that an education technology improved student learning?

- Define the technology and the outcome (10 min)
- Read, rate, and present nine anonymized studies (35 min)
- Reveal who produced them; discuss (10 min)
- Agree on five evidence standards (20 min)

::: notes
Put the question on the board in these words and leave it there. Every activity returns to it.

The product of the class is five standards, written by the students and voted on, that a district could use. Say that now so the group work has a destination.
:::

# Poll 1: What Counts as Education Technology?

In one sentence, on your phone: what counts as education technology?

Join: steffenerickson.github.io/edtech-evidence-lab · Session code on the board

::: notes
Phase: defining. Give two minutes. Turn on "students can see results" and read four or five definitions aloud from the dashboard, choosing ones that disagree: one that includes any device, one that limits to instructional software, one that mentions AI.

The point to draw out: the breadth of the definition determines which evidence counts. A study of home computers is evidence about one definition and not another.
:::

# A Working Definition

Education technology: tools designed to support student learning.

- Learning apps and practice platforms
- Personalized or adaptive learning software
- Technology-mediated tutoring
- AI-enabled learning tools

Not today: devices in general, administrative systems, screen time as such.

::: notes
State the definition and the exclusions. AI is included but is not the focus; two of the nine studies are about AI tools.

One study in the set is about general screen time and one is about home computers. They are included so the class can ask whether evidence about the broader category transfers to purposeful instructional tools.
:::

# Which Outcome?

Possible outcomes of education technology:

- Standardized test achievement
- Course grades and completion
- Engagement, attendance, time on task
- Skills the tests do not measure, including skills with the technology itself
- Teacher workload and practice

Today: achievement on standardized tests.

::: notes
Ask for two or three other outcomes before showing the list. Then restrict to standardized test achievement and say plainly what that costs: a technology could raise engagement or teach skills the tests do not measure and still show nothing here. Study E, the home computer study, found both lower grades and higher computer skills, which is the cleanest example of the restriction mattering.
:::

# The Activity

Groups of two or three. Each group has two studies on the Studies page, marked "your group."

1. Read both studies (12 minutes).
2. For each study, agree on the **direction of findings**: positive, negative, mixed, null, or inconclusive.
3. Decide how **credible** the study is, and what you are unsure about (8 minutes).
4. Each group presents both studies in two minutes.

You may submit your individual ratings on the Rate page at any point before the lock.

::: notes
Phase: reading. Credibility is deliberately undefined. When a group asks what it means, say: that is part of the exercise; decide what it means to you and tell the class.

Studies are anonymized. There are no author names, products, or publications. Students will see those after the first vote.

While they read, watch the join count and the rating count on the dashboard.
:::

# Group Presentations

Two minutes per group:

- What was studied and how
- Direction of findings
- How credible, and why
- The one thing you most wanted to know and could not

::: notes
Phase: rating_pre. Keep strict time. Write each group's "one thing you wanted to know" on the board; most will be about who did the study, how it was funded, or whether anyone replicated it. Those become standards later.

Remind everyone to submit individual ratings on the Rate page before the lock.
:::

# Poll 2: Rate Before the Reveal

On the Rate page, for each of your two studies:

- Direction: positive / negative / mixed / null / inconclusive
- Credibility: 1 (low) to 5 (high)
- One sentence of reason, optional

Ratings lock when this step closes.

::: notes
Give 90 seconds, then press **locked** on the dashboard. The lock is enforced in the database: after this, a pre-reveal rating cannot be added or changed from any phone.

Do not advance to "revealed" until the lock is set. The dashboard asks for confirmation if you try.
:::

# The Reveal

[Live: citations, authors, affiliations, funders, and relationships for all eleven sources]

Open the Studies page: each card now shows its source.

::: notes
Phase: revealed. Give students a minute to scroll their own two cards. Then walk through four contrasts.

B and F. Study B is a correlational dosage analysis written by employees of the platform's maker, cited on its sales page, and never peer reviewed. Study F is a randomized trial of 17,000 students by independent evaluators, funded by foundations that also fund tutoring. Which concern is bigger: company authorship, or funders with a stake in the answer?

A and N. Study A is the OECD report: a hill-shaped correlation that the report itself says cannot be read causally. N is the BBC headline: "Computers 'do not improve' pupil results." Same evidence, different claim.

G and H. Both are randomized trials of the same kind of AI chatbot, both from 2025, both independent of the vendor. G found 0.23 standard deviations in English after six weeks of supervised sessions. H found a 17 percent drop on the unassisted exam. Independence did not produce agreement; the designs asked different questions.

:::

# After the Reveal

[Live: pre-reveal credibility distributions by study, now with sources known]

::: notes
Ask which ratings students would change now, and why. Expect B to fall in their estimation and F to hold or rise.

Two cautions, said plainly. First, a conflict of interest is a reason for scrutiny, not dismissal. Study B's design would be weak with any author; the company authorship tells you where to look, not what you will find. Second, independence is not causality. Study A has no vendor anywhere near it and still cannot support a causal claim. The question is always what the design can identify, and then who had reasons to shade it.
:::

# Poll 3: Propose a Standard

On the Standards page, one sentence: a standard the class should use to decide whether a study, or a body of studies, is credible evidence that an education technology changed student learning.

::: notes
Phase: standards_submit. Three minutes. One per student. Watch the count reach the number of students joined.
:::

# Sorting the Standards

Two kinds of standards:

- **For a single study.** Example: a credible comparison group.
- **For the evidence base.** Example: independent replication; publication of null results.

::: notes
Phase: standards_sort. Read submissions from the dashboard. Create a ballot option for each distinct idea, merge duplicates by assigning submissions to the same option, and mark the scope. Six minutes.

Name the distinction: a single study can be excellent and the evidence base still thin. Most student submissions will be single-study standards; ask whether anyone proposed one for the evidence base, and if not, whether the class wants one on the ballot.
:::

# Vote

On the Standards page, choose up to five.

::: notes
Phase: standards_vote. Four minutes. The database rejects more than five selections. Resubmitting replaces the earlier ballot.
:::

# Our Five Standards

[Live: the five standards with the most votes]

::: notes
Phase: final. Tick the five winners on the dashboard. If there is a tie at fifth place, show the tied options, take a voice vote, and tick the winner.

For each standard, one sentence on why it matters, using a study from today as the example. Then two questions: what did we leave out, and how would a district use these five when a vendor presents a study?

End on the five. Do not add a summary slide after this.
:::
